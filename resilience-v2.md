# Background
On October 13, 2022, Vance Russell and Elizabeth Schuster published a storymap called "WTF is Resilience, Anyway? Applying Resilience to Conservation Landscapes". This is an updated version of that article.

# Resilience
In the early 2010s, Vance spent the better part of a day looking at postfire reforestation efforts following the Moonlight Fire, California. The 263 km2 (65,000 acres) fire went on for miles and was one of the largest in the state at the time. It seems small now, compared to recent fires of more than one million acres. The landscape was a mosaic of recovering stands, especially in wetter north-facing slopes that were recovering on their own. Still, other areas were not recovering as quickly (high-intensity burn areas, south-facing, or far away from seed sources). In those less resilient areas, active restoration was needed for forest recovery. 

You might think you know what resilience is, but chances are it’s an oversimplified definition. Resilience has become a buzzword in recent years but often is conflated to mean bouncing back after a shock. While that is part of the definition, this  can lead us to design ineffective strategies and interventions. The solution is to get back to the true definition of ecological resilience. When we develop solutions and interventions that mimic natural systems, we’ll be more successful at achieving resilience—for nature AND communities.  

This post brings the definition of resilience to life in a way that can be used by conservation managers, communities, and anyone who cares about the health of forests. Then we offer case studies of what resilience looks like in real life.  

## Definition
Ecologists and land managers frequently use the concept of resilience. For some, it is a combination of resilience and resistance, where resilience is the ability of an ecosystem to experience disturbance and then return to its pre-disturbance condition. Resistance is the ability of an ecosystem to withstand disturbance without any measurable ecosystem changes. @brand offer a useful resilience synthesis with an organizing typology, noting the tension between the original ecological descriptive definition of resilience with the more recent, vague, and malleable notions of resilience. A useful visualization of the degrees of resilience changes as a function of tree stress and disturbance ({numref}`resil-diag`).

@north-resilience argue that many dry western U.S. forests beyond the Sierra Nevada had frequent, low-intensity fire regimes and that thinning for restoring forest resilience may need to be more intensive than current fuels reduction approaches. They suggest that forest health resilience can be measured by a metric called the stand density index (a measure of the relationship between average tree size and trees/unit area).

## Framework
The Tahoe Central Sierra Initiative aims to accelerate large-landscape forest restoration to improve the health and resilience of the Sierra Nevada. It focuses on developing and demonstrating innovative planning, investment, and management tools across a 2.4-million-acre landscape.

Partners across the landscape developed a framework for resilience to inform the restoration of social and ecological resilience across the landscape (hatched polygons). The Framework is becoming widely accepted across California for measuring landscape resilience.

The [Framework for Resilience](https://www.tahoecentralsierra.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/psw-tcsi-frameworkforresilience.pdf) consists of 10 pillars of
resilience focused on forest resilience, carbon sequestration, fire dynamics,
fire-adapted communities, economic diversity, social and cultural well-being,
wetland integrity, biodiversity conservation, water
security, and air quality. The framework and assessment will be used to
identify projects with the greatest benefit to resilience across the region.

## Burney-Hat creek Collaborative
map of the projects

The Burney-Hat Creek collaborative, located in NE California, is inventorying forest health projects across the region. The goal of this work is to understand how treatments are mitigating wildfire across the region, analyze the biomass quantities produced by projects, and leverage public and private funds to increase project pace and scale. The collaborative is piloting a [Forest Health Fund] to raise $30 million in public and private funds and facilitate the connections between private investors and public funding sources.

## Forest Health
At first glance, forest resilience might sound like something that only matters in areas where severe drought and wildfires are a big issue. Many of us are familiar with the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) label on our paper towels, furniture, and other products. If that label already tells us that we can be reassured about forests being managed responsibly and will last long into the future, what more do we need?

The reality is that across the United States, forest health is at risk: from land conversion, blight, invasive plants and insects, and climate change. One report from New England noted that climate change is already negatively impacting forests due to higher daytime temperatures, shorter winters, and more intense rain and storm (Catanzaro 2016). In fact climate change is increasing the speed and impact of threats to forest worldwide and a no-action response or accepting that high severity fires are a norm is a mistaken and scientifically unjustified approach to forest health.

And the true definition of ecological resilience does not have to be an abstract, academic concept. There are tangible actions landowners can take immediately to improve the resilience of their forests. Highlights from the Catanzaro report include creating a management plan for your forest, increasing diversity in tree age classes, species, and snags, working across the landscape with neighbors, and creating a well-defined measurement program to gauge success over time. 

Managing forest resilience is not a luxury or icing on the cake for those who may have additional time and resources to experiment with different techniques.

We can no longer afford to keep resilient strategies as fringe or abstract concepts that don’t have real-life applicability.

Forest resilience is a crucial component of forest management that will be necessary to ensure that our ecosystems and forest industries survive and thrive under climate change.
